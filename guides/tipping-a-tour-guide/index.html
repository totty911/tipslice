Tipping a tour guide

By Matt Totty, TipSlice · Last reviewed November 13, 2026

A tour guide is not a restaurant server, and the percent on a dinner check is the wrong tool until you have already chosen a dollar amount. Emily Post publishes two wide dollar ranges, not a single percent of the ticket. Visit London publishes a smaller per-person range in pounds, and it treats a free walking tour differently from a paid one. Japan, Mexico, and Ontario mention a guide without a number. Where a page prints no amount, this guide does not supply one.

What Emily Post prints

The February 6, 2025 article Etiquette Today: Travel Tipping, from Lizzie and Dan at the Emily Post Institute, gives the tour-guide line as "$5 to $50 or possibly $50 to $500 for multi-city or country tour guides." The next sentences split that into two cases. For a short tour you just picked up, $5 to $50 is the range they call great, with more if you are a group or the guide did a great job. For a guide who accompanies you through multiple cities or countries, the range is $50 to $500. Give it at the end of the tour or the trip, with a thank-you.

The general tipping chart does not have a tour-guide row. It does have a concierge row, which is a different job: no obligation for answering questions, $5 to $10 for tickets or restaurant reservations, and $15 for hard-to-get tickets, or 10 to 20 percent of the ticket price. Do not paste the concierge percent onto a walking tour. A guide who only answered where the museum entrance is has not become the concierge line either. The travel article's own concierge sentence is $5 to $20 for a reservation or hard-to-get tickets, and it says you do not tip every easy question.

"More if you're a group" is not a second published table. A $10 tip is inside the $5 to $50 short-tour range. Forty dollars, from four people putting in $10 each, is still inside $50. Eighty dollars, from four people putting in $20 each, is above $50. That $80 is allowed by the phrase about a group or a great job. It is not a required $20 per person. The article never prints a per-person short-tour rate. The $50 to $500 band is the multi-city or multi-country guide, given once at the end, not $50 to $500 per city.

A worked example that stays inside the printed range

You finished a two-hour paid tour and you want a tip inside $5 to $50. Ten dollars is inside that range. So is $25. So is $50. None of those three is "the" Emily Post tip. They are three points you can check before you hand over cash.

The calculator is still useful after you pick a percent of the ticket, because you need to see whether the result lands inside the dollar range. Ten percent is not an Emily Post tour-guide rate. It is a test. Ten percent of an $80 ticket is $8, which is inside $5 to $50. Twenty percent of $80 is $16, also inside. Twenty-five percent of a $240 private tour is $60, which is above the short-tour $50 and therefore only fits the article if you are treating the tour as a group, a great job, or a multi-city guide. Open $80 at 10 percent, $80 at 20 percent, and $240 at 25 percent. Read the tip line, then compare it with $5, $50, and $500. Do not compare it with a restaurant's 15 to 20 percent and call the comparison a rule.

London

Visit London says tipping is not mandatory, and its table lists tour guides as optional at £5 to £10 per person. The tour-guide section says it is common to tip, that free walking tours in particular rely on tips so tipping is expected, and that a good range is £5 to £10 per person. It also says you can usually find a suggested amount on the tour operator's website. That suggested amount is the one to follow when it exists. This page will not average it with £5 to £10.

Two people at £8 each is £16. Four people at £5 each is £20. Four people at £10 each is £40. Those are the table's edges times the size of the party, not a percent of a London dinner. VisitBritain's useful-information page does not print a tour-guide amount. Its 10 to 15 percent sentence is for a restaurant when nothing was added to the bill. Restaurant service charges are the UK guide. A free walking tour that names £10 on its own site is £10 because the operator said so, even though £10 is also the top of Visit London's range.

Japan

The Japan National Tourism Organization says tipping is not common at hotels, restaurants, cafes, bars, or taxis. The exception it names is a private guide or interpreter who is accustomed to overseas practice. They may accept a tip as thanks. It is not expected, and it should be done discreetly. If you offer it, the custom on that page is an envelope, the kind sold at convenience stores, stationery shops, or 100-yen shops. The page does not print a yen amount. Do not convert Emily Post's $5 to $50 into yen and call the result a JNTO figure. The restaurant and taxi refusals are the Japan guide.

Mexico and Ontario

Mexico's tourism secretariat says small tips are appreciated for tour guides, in the same sentence as hotel housekeeping, and that 10 to 15 percent is common in restaurants. It does not print a separate guide amount in pesos or dollars. VisitMexico puts 10 to 15 percent on meals and drinks, and "a few coins" on porters, cab drivers, and cleaning staff. A tour guide is not in that coin sentence. Using the meal percent, or the coins, as the guide's tip would be a guess. The meal figures stay on the Mexico guide.

Destination Ontario, updated October 28, 2026, says tips are also given to tour guides, taxi drivers, and for spa treatments and haircuts, after saying 15 to 20 percent is customary at bars and restaurants. The guide is named. No dollar figure is attached. The porter line on that page is $1 or $2 per bag, which is not a guide. Restaurant and taxi ranges for Canada are the Canada guide. Bonjour Québec's 10 to 15 percent before tax is a restaurant, bar, and taxi sentence. It does not mention a tour guide.

Australia, and the end of the tour

Tourism Australia says tipping is your choice, it is not expected in most venues, and 10 percent for good service is common in upmarket restaurants. The page does not mention tour guides. A 10 percent restaurant habit is not evidence of a 10 percent guide habit in Australia. If you still want to see what 10 percent of a ticket is, the calculator will do the cents, and you then decide whether that cent result belongs anywhere. It does not belong in a sentence that claims Tourism Australia asked for it.

Pay the guide at the end, which is when the Emily Post article says to hand it over, unless the operator collected a gratuity with the booking. Cash is what you can count against a dollar range on the spot. A card prompt for 15, 20, or 25 percent is the terminal's list, not the travel article's list. If you use the prompt, convert it first. Fifteen percent of a $90 ticket is $13.50, inside $5 to $50. Twenty-five percent of $90 is $22.50, also inside. Fifteen percent of a $900 multi-city fee is $135, inside $50 to $500. Twenty-five percent of $900 is $225, also inside. Open $90 at 15 percent only as that test. Thank the person. The article treats the thank-you as part of the tip, not as a substitute it priced.

Tour guide FAQ

Is there one percent for a tour guide?

Not in the Emily Post travel article. A short tour is $5 to $50, more for a group or a great job. A multi-city or multi-country guide is $50 to $500 at the end. A percent is only a way to see if your result sits inside those dollars. Visit London's own range is £5 to £10 per person, and the operator's posted suggestion wins when there is one.

Should I tip a guide in Japan?

JNTO says a private guide or interpreter used to overseas practice may accept a discreet envelope, and that the tip is not expected. Ordinary hotels, restaurants, and taxis are not that case, and the page prints no yen amount.
