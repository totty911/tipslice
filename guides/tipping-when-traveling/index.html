Tipping when traveling

By Matt Totty, TipSlice · Last updated October 17, 2026

A 20% U.S. restaurant tip is a local custom tied to local wage rules. It is not a global minimum, and applying it unchanged in a country where service is already in the price can mean paying twice or handing someone money they are expected to refuse. This page compares the United States with Canada, the United Kingdom, France, Germany, Japan, and Australia, using each country's own public guidance. It also lists the limits of that guidance, because a chart from a tourism office loses to the bill on your table.

Rules that travel better than percentages

  1. Read the bill before you add anything. Look for service, servizio, service compris, a cover charge, or a gratuity. A line that is already included is not a suggestion.
  2. Ask if the words are unclear. "Is service included?" is a normal question. The answer beats a blog post you read on the plane.
  3. Do not treat a tourist-area habit as the whole country. A restaurant that serves mostly visitors may print a U.S.-style tip line even where local custom is lighter.
  4. Cash and cards are not always equal. Some places cannot add a tip to a card, or the card tip is a service charge the house controls. If you want a specific person to receive money, ask how that works there.
  5. Refusing a tip is sometimes the polite outcome. Insisting, especially in Japan, can create the awkwardness you were trying to avoid.

The percentages below are starting points from the cited pages, checked when this guide was updated. Only the French note describes a legal rule. When a source and a menu disagree, follow the menu and ask.

United States

For sit-down service, the Emily Post Institute lists 15% to 20% of the pre-tax bill. That range sits next to a federal rule, explained in Labor Department Fact Sheet #15, that lets many employers pay a $2.13 cash wage if tips make up the rest of the $7.25 minimum. Several states require a full cash minimum instead. The restaurant guide has the state table. Plan on 15% to 20% at a U.S. sit-down meal, watch for an automatic gratuity on large parties, and do not assume a coffee counter expects the same percentage.

Six other countries

Canada The standard tip is usually 15% of the bill. Tipping for good service is common in restaurants, bars, taxis, and hotels. Government of Canada, settling in Canada: money
United Kingdom Tipping is not expected the way it is in some countries. Staff must be paid at least the National Minimum Wage. An optional service charge is sometimes added, and you can choose whether to pay it. If nothing is added, 10% to 15% for good restaurant service is well received. France Service is a required percentage included in the price. Displayed prices are what you pay, service included. A tip (pourboire) is optional and no occupation can demand it. Where service is charged, menus must say "prix service compris" and the percentage. France's tourism site adds that prices usually include tax and a 15% service charge, and that another 2% to 3% is customary when a meal was particularly good. DGCCRF, Pourboire and France.fr, money
Germany Service is generally included in restaurant prices. It is customary to leave about 5% to 10%. An earlier national tourism guide for U.S. travelers said bills include VAT and service, and that 10% is common. German National Tourist Board, 2026 resource guide (PDF)
Japan Tipping is not common for bars, cafes, restaurants, taxis, or hotels, and it can cause discomfort. A service charge is often already on a restaurant bill. A discreet gratuity (kokorozuke), in an envelope, may be accepted by a private guide or interpreter who is used to overseas clients. It is not expected. Japan National Tourism Organization, tipping in Japan
Australia Hotels and restaurants do not add a service charge, and tipping is your choice. In most places it is not expected. In upmarket restaurants, 10% for good service is common. Tourism Australia, key facts

Read the cells as the source wrote them. Canada's 15% is "usually," not a required add-on. Britain's 10% to 15% is what VisitBritain calls well received when nothing was already added. France is the strict row: the displayed price includes service, and a tip cannot be demanded. Germany separates included service from an extra 5% to 10%. Japan is a refusal case with a narrow guide exception. Australia treats tipping as a choice, with 10% common only in upmarket rooms.

Caveats that matter more than the chart

A printed service charge wins. In Britain, VisitBritain says an optional service charge may already be on the bill and that you can decline it. In France, "service compris" means you are not looking at a missing tip. In Japan, JNTO notes that a service charge is generally added. Adding a U.S. 20% on top of any of those, without reading, is the expensive mistake. If a charge is optional, decide whether the service earned it. If it is mandatory, an extra coin or two is a compliment, not a second bill.

A cover charge is not a tip. A per-person cover, sometimes called coperto, is a fee for the table. Do not treat it as the gratuity, and do not subtract it from a tip you decided to leave.

Tourist corridors drift toward American habits. A blank tip line in a city where tipping is light does not create a 20% debt. Follow the local source above, and leave a smaller thank-you only if you mean to and the bill did not already include one.

Wage context is not the same. VisitBritain notes that British staff must receive at least the National Minimum Wage and do not have to live on tips. That is a different structure from the U.S. federal tip credit of $2.13 in Fact Sheet #15. The July 1, 2026 DOL table also shows several U.S. states requiring a full cash minimum before tips. Do not export one country's wage story into another.

Tax is often already in the menu price. The German tourist board notes that restaurant prices include tax, and French displayed prices include service. A U.S. menu price often does not. The pre-tax habit is a U.S. habit. Abroad, read the line before you multiply.

Private guides are a separate case. JNTO allows a discreet envelope for a private guide or interpreter used to overseas clients. Ask the tour operator what their people expect. Countries not in the table are not "like France" or "like the U.S." by default. Check that country's own tourism or government page, then check the bill.

What the percentages cost on $60

On a 60 food total, 5% is 3, 10% is 6, 15% is 9, and 20% is 12. The gap between a service-included meal and a U.S. 20% tip is most of that 12.

Two people, $60, at 10% and at 20%

At 10%, the tip is $6.00 and the total is $66.00, or $33.00 each. At 20%, the tip is $12.00 and the total is $72.00, or $36.00 each. Use 10% as a stand-in for the lighter end of the British, German, and Australian figures above. Use 20% for a U.S. sit-down meal at the top of the Emily Post range. Neither link adds sales tax. Add &x= and your rate if you are tipping in a place where tax is not already in the price.
