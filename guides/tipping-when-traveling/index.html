Tipping when traveling

By Matt Totty, TipSlice · Last updated October 19, 2026

A 20% U.S. restaurant tip is a local custom tied to local wage rules. It is not a global minimum, and applying it unchanged in a country where service is already in the price can mean paying twice or handing someone money they are expected to refuse. This page compares the United States with Canada, the United Kingdom, France, Germany, Japan, and Australia, using each country's own public guidance. It also lists the limits of that guidance, because a chart from a tourism office loses to the bill on your table.

Rules that travel better than percentages

  1. Read the bill before you add anything. Look for service, servizio, service compris, a cover charge, or a gratuity. A line that is already included is not a suggestion.
  2. Ask if the words are unclear. "Is service included?" is a normal question. The answer beats a blog post you read on the plane.
  3. Do not treat a tourist-area habit as the whole country. A restaurant that serves mostly visitors may print a U.S.-style tip line even where local custom is lighter.
  4. Cash and cards are not always equal. Some places cannot add a tip to a card, or the card tip is a service charge the house controls. If you want a specific person to receive money, ask how that works there.
  5. A Tokyo restaurant will almost certainly refuse a tip. That sentence is from the city's official visitor guide, not a general rule that insisting is rude everywhere.

The percentages below are starting points from the cited pages, checked when this guide was updated. The France guide, the UK guide, the Germany guide, and the Italy guide quote the legal and tourism pages. When a source and a menu disagree, follow the menu and ask.

United States

For sit-down service, the Emily Post Institute lists 15% to 20% of the pre-tax bill. That range sits next to a federal rule, explained in Labor Department Fact Sheet #15, that lets many employers pay a $2.13 cash wage if tips make up the rest of the $7.25 minimum. Several states require a full cash minimum instead. The tipped minimum wage calculator copies the July 1, 2026 table.

Caveats that matter more than the chart

A printed service charge wins. In Britain, VisitBritain says an optional service charge may already be on the bill and that you can decline it. The UK guide quotes HMRC on when that charge is voluntary. In France, "service compris" means you are not looking at a missing tip. The France guide has the menu wording. Tokyo's visitor guide says an izakaya often adds an otoshi appetizer as a cover charge. That line is not a tip. Adding a U.S. 20% on top of a charge you did not read is the expensive mistake. If a charge is optional, decide whether the service earned it. If it is mandatory, an extra coin or two is a compliment, not a second bill.

A cover charge is not a tip. Italy's education ministry says most restaurants and pizzerias charge a fixed fee called coperto, and that a tip is welcome but not an obligation. Lazio Regional Law 22/2019, Article 75, forbids an additional coperto charge. Rome is in Lazio. The Italy guide quotes Italia.it, the ministry page, and that statute.

A blank tip line is not a 20% debt. Follow the source in the table, and leave something extra only if you mean to and the bill did not already include it.

Wage context is not the same. VisitBritain notes that British staff must receive at least the National Minimum Wage and do not have to live on tips. That is a different structure from the U.S. federal tip credit of $2.13 in Fact Sheet #15. The July 1, 2026 DOL table also shows several U.S. states requiring a full cash minimum before tips. Do not export one country's wage story into another.

Tax is often already in the menu price. The German tourist board notes that restaurant prices include tax, and French displayed prices include service. A U.S. menu price often does not. The pre-tax habit is a U.S. habit. Abroad, read the line before you multiply.

Private guides are a separate case. JNTO says a private guide or interpreter used to overseas practice may accept a discreet envelope, and that it is not expected. The Japan guide has the rest. Countries not in the table need their own source.

What the percentages cost on $60

On a 60 food total, 5% is 3, 10% is 6, 15% is 9, and 20% is 12. The gap between a service-included meal and a U.S. 20% tip is most of that 12.
