Tipping on the pre-tax or post-tax total

By Matt Totty, TipSlice · Last reviewed October 19, 2026

Sales tax is not a tip, and it is not payment for service. The traditional U.S. habit is to figure the tip on the food-and-drink subtotal, before tax. Tipping on the after-tax total is a bit more money and a bit less arithmetic, which is why card machines and tired tables do it. Both are understandable. They are not the same number. This page uses a published city tax rate so you can see the gap in dollars, then shows the TipSlice switch that calculates either way.

What etiquette says

The Emily Post Institute chart states the sit-down range as 15% to 20%, pre-tax. The Institute's finer points say it is acceptable to tip on the pre-tax amount of the bill, and that for large parties this can be quite different than the total. The two methods are not the same number. The tables below use published city rates so the gap is in dollars.

That standard is etiquette, not a statute. If the table prefers the after-tax number because it is the only figure on the receipt, use it and say so. The mistake is assuming the two methods match.

The dollars on an $80 check

Take a pre-tax food-and-drink bill of $80.00 and a combined sales tax rate of 8.875%, which is New York City's published combined rate (more on that rate below). Tax is $80.00 times 0.08875, which is $7.10. The after-tax total is $87.10.

A 20% tip on the pre-tax bill is $16.00. A 20% tip on the after-tax total is 20% of $87.10, which is $17.42. The difference is $1.42 on this check. The guest who tips after tax is not tipping 20% for service. They are tipping 20% on the meal and another 20% on the tax, which is $1.42 of extra tip, equal to 1.775% of the meal. Their effective tip on the food is 21.775%.

Each person's gap on this check is $0.71. On a $240 party check at the same 8.875% rate, tax is $21.30, 20% before tax is $48.00, and 20% after tax is $52.26. The gap is $4.26.

The extra tip from using the after-tax total equals your tip percentage times the tax dollars. At 15% on this New York check the gap is $1.07. At 18% it is $1.28.

Where the tax rate comes from

U.S. sales tax is a stack of state, city, and special-district rates, and restaurants in two neighborhoods can differ. Do not memorize a national number. Use the rate printed on that receipt.

New York City's combined sales and use tax rate is 8.875%: New York State 4%, New York City 4.5%, and the Metropolitan Commuter Transportation District 0.375%. The state Department of Taxation and Finance publishes local rates in its sales tax rate lookup and jurisdiction tables. The $7.10 in the example is that city rate applied to $80, not a guess.

California's statewide base is 7.25%, and cities and districts add to it. The California Department of Tax and Fee Administration explains the state rate and local add-ons on its sales and use tax rates page. Twenty percent of the tax on an $80 meal at the 7.25% base is 20% of $5.80, which is $1.16. In a city where the total rate is higher, the post-tax tip rises with it. The sales tax calculator adds or removes a rate you type in. TipSlice does not look up your location, because a stale built-in table would be worse than the number on your receipt.

Some receipts already show one tax-inclusive total and hide the subtotal in a fold or a second screen. If you want a pre-tax tip and you only have the grand total, you can back the tax out. At 8.875%, divide the after-tax amount by 1.08875. An $87.10 total divided by 1.08875 returns $80.00. Rounding at the register can leave you a cent off. Treat the result as the subtotal to tip on, then check it against the itemized bill if you have one.

The calculator toggle

The TipSlice tip calculator defaults to a pre-tax tip. Sales tax is off until you turn it on. When you turn it on, you type the rate, and a checkbox labeled "Tip on the after-tax total" switches the base. Leave the box unchecked for the etiquette default. Check it if you want the tip figured on food plus tax.

The share link records that choice. These are the query keys the calculator reads:

  • b is the bill, the pre-tax food and drinks when you are also sending a tax rate.
  • p is the number of people.
  • t is the tip percent.
  • x is the tax percent. If x is present, sales tax is on.
  • o=1 means tip on the after-tax total. It only applies together with x.

A link with x and no o is the pre-tax tip. A link with x and o=1 is the post-tax tip. Both examples below use $80, two people, 20%, and 8.875% tax, so the only change is that flag. The calculator works in cents and rounds the half-cent in the usual way. On this example the tax is an exact $7.10 and the tips are exact, so you will not see a rounding note. When a group does not divide evenly, each person's share is rounded up to the next cent. That rule is explained in the split-the-bill guide.

Groups, screens, and discounts

Payment screens often compute suggested tips on the amount about to be charged, which is the after-tax total, sometimes with a service charge already inside it. If you tap 20% on that screen, you have chosen the post-tax method even if you meant the other one. If you care, calculate first and type a custom tip. The pre-tax 20% on the $80 example is $16.00, not the $17.42 the screen may offer.

Agree on the base before anyone starts sending money. "Twenty percent" is not specific until you say of what. The person holding the card should be reimbursed for the method the table picked, including tax and tip. Leaving the tip as that person's private cost is how tables under-tip without noticing.

Discounts are a different base question. A coupon, happy-hour price already reflected in the subtotal, or a comped dessert should not shrink the tip if the server did the full job. Tip on the pre-discount food total when you know it. A comp that never appears as a dollar amount is harder. If the menu price of the comped dish was $14, add $14 back to the tip base before you take 18% or 20%. Tax still stays out of that base if you are following the pre-tax habit.

A mandatory gratuity is not a tax and not a voluntary tip. Do not run 20% on a total that already includes 18% auto-gratuity unless you intend to leave both. How to read that line is the subject of the large-party guide.
