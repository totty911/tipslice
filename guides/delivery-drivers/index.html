Tipping delivery drivers

By Matt Totty, TipSlice · Last updated October 17, 2026

A delivery tip pays for the trip, not for the restaurant's cooking. The driver used a vehicle, waited at the store, and carried the order to your door. A percentage of the food is a useful starting point. On a small order it is often too small, which is why a dollar floor belongs in the same decision. This page covers restaurant delivery, pizza, and grocery drop-off in the United States, and it separates the tip line from the fees an app adds on its own.

How much to leave

The Emily Post Institute chart lists a delivery tip of 10% to 15% of the bill, and $2 to $5 for pizza depending on the size of the order and how difficult the drop-off is. A 2025 Institute note on everyday tipping, adapted from Emily Post's Etiquette, puts grocery delivery and restaurant takeout delivery at about 10%, or $5 or more per order, and curbside pickup at a couple of dollars ($5 for a large load). Those two statements are easy to use together: start around 10% to 15% of the pre-tax food total, and do not let the result fall below a few dollars on a small order.

Fifteen percent is a solid default for ordinary restaurant delivery in fair weather, to a door the driver can reach without a hike. Twenty percent fits a hard trip. Ten percent fits a short, easy drop when the order is large enough that 10% is already a meaningful amount. Pickup at the restaurant is not delivery. Ordinary takeout has no obligation on the same chart, with 10% reserved for a large or complicated order or for curb service. That distinction is in the restaurant tipping guide.

Tip on the food and drinks, before delivery fees, service fees, and tax. The driver did not provide the sales tax, and a fee the company set is not part of the meal they carried. If you are unsure which line is food, open the charge breakdown before you apply the percentage. The pre-tax guide shows why the base you choose changes the dollars.

The small-order floor

Percentages fail on cheap orders. Fifteen percent of an $18 salad is $2.70. Twenty percent is $3.60. Either figure can be less than the cost of the driver's time and mileage, especially if the restaurant was not next door. A floor of about $5 on a small food order matches the spirit of the Institute's "$5 or more" note and is easier to remember than a percentage that comes out to pocket change.

Use the higher of the percentage and the floor. On $18, the floor wins: leave $5, not $2.70. On a $42 order, 15% is $6.30, which is already above $5, so the percentage wins. On a $70 family order, 15% is $10.50 and 20% is $14.00. The floor is irrelevant there. Pizza follows the same idea with the chart's $2 to $5 range as the small-order language: a single pizza dropped at a house on the way can be the low end; three pizzas, an apartment with no elevator, or a storm is the high end or more.

Fees that are not the tip

App checkouts stack lines that look like generosity and are not. Read the labels.

  • Tip, or gratuity. This is the amount you intend for the driver. It is the line this page is about.
  • Delivery fee. A charge for the trip, set by the restaurant or the app. Do not assume it is passed through as pay.
  • Service fee. A company charge. It is not a tip, even when the screen places it near the tip buttons.
  • Small-order fee, busy-area fee, or regulatory fee. These are pricing. They do not replace a tip.

The screen will not always say, in the moment, what share of a fee reaches the driver. The reliable move is to treat every fee as not-the-tip, then set the tip line on purpose. If a restaurant's own site adds a "courier benefit" or similar line, read that label the same way. A compulsory charge is not a tip under federal tax rules, even if the receipt uses the word tip. The IRS explains the difference in Publication 531 and on its tip recordkeeping page: a tip is voluntary, the customer sets the amount (including zero), and the customer generally decides who receives it. An automatic charge fails that test.

Suggested buttons of 15%, 20%, and 25%, sometimes calculated on a total that already includes fees, are defaults. You can edit the amount. Choosing a lower custom number, or a flat $5, is a normal use of the screen.

Weather, stairs, and distance

Add to the usual range when the trip is harder than a flat drop at a front door.

  • Weather. Rain, snow, ice, and extreme heat are part of the job that night. Moving from 15% toward 20%, or adding a few dollars to the floor, is the straightforward adjustment.
  • Stairs and access. A walk-up, a building with a broken elevator, a gated complex that takes ten minutes to enter, or a drop that requires the driver to leave the car far from the door all justify more than the base percentage.
  • Distance. A long drive from the restaurant is not reflected in the food price. If you know the store is across town, do not use the same tip you would for a place two blocks away.
  • Size and care. Cases of water, catering trays, and orders with many drinks are heavier and slower. Grocery orders with a lot of bags fit the "large load" case in the Emily Post note.

A late kitchen or a missing item is a refund issue, not a reason to zero the tip. The trip still happened. Curbside pickup is lighter: a couple of dollars, or about $5 for a large load.

Cash, the app, and taxes

Cash handed to the driver is simple and arrives immediately. A tip entered in the app is easier to track and is the right tool when you do not have cash. If you do both, know that you did both. Do not add 20% in the app and another 20% at the door unless you mean to.

Tips are taxable. IRS Publication 531, written for employees, says all tips are income and that $20 or more in a month from one job must be reported to the employer by the 10th of the next month. Many delivery drivers are contractors, so that employee step does not map one for one, but the income still belongs on the return. The IRS tip page says a company service charge paid out as wages is not a voluntary tip.

A median wage for couriers will not price one trip. Many app drivers are self-employed, and the Bureau of Labor Statistics occupational wage series generally omits the self-employed. Decide from the food total, the floor, and the difficulty of the drop.
