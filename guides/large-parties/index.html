Large parties and automatic gratuity

By Matt Totty, TipSlice · Last updated October 17, 2026

Large parties get an automatic charge because a table of eight is harder to serve and easier to under-tip when everyone assumes someone else handled it. The charge is often about 18%, often starts around six to eight guests, and is often mandatory. The etiquette problem is simple: do not pay it twice. The tax problem is less obvious: under IRS rules that 18% is usually a service charge, not a tip, even when the receipt prints it on a line labeled "tip."

What restaurants usually add

There is no single U.S. law that says "parties of six pay 18%." The policy is the restaurant's, and it should be on the menu or the reservation note if the house wants you to know before you sit down. In practice you will see a threshold of six, seven, or eight people, and a percentage of 18% or 20% of food and drink. Some rooms apply it only at dinner, or only if you booked as a party. Ask when you reserve. "Do you add a gratuity for a party of eight?" is a complete question.

If nothing is added, the Emily Post Institute range is still 15% to 20% of the pre-tax bill. Agree on 18% or 20% before the check arrives. The restaurant guide covers an ordinary table. This page is about the printed charge.

On a banquet contract, a service charge and an administrative fee can be different lines. Ask in writing who receives each one before the event.

How to read the bill

Find the line before anyone taps a tip button. Useful phrases: gratuity, auto gratuity, service charge, party gratuity, and "gratuity included." Then look at the math. Eighteen percent of the food and drinks should be close to the dollars printed. If the check shows food of $240 and a gratuity of $43.20, that is 18% and it is already there.

Three outcomes follow:

  • The charge is mandatory and it is the gratuity. You do not add another 18% or 20%. An extra few dollars, or another 2% to 5% for service that was clearly better than the policy assumed, is optional.
  • The charge is optional. You can change it or remove it. If you remove it, leave an ordinary 15% to 20% yourself. Walking out with neither the printed charge nor a tip is a zero, not a correction.
  • The wording does not say whether staff receive it. Ask. A service charge can be retained by the business. The IRS says so in plain language, below. Asking is not rude when the menu used a vague label.

Card tablets make the double tip easy. The suggested buttons may calculate 18%, 20%, and 25% on a total that already includes the automatic 18%. Tapping 20% on that screen is not "a little extra." On a $240 food bill it is another $48, for a combined $91.20, which is 38% of the food. Type a custom amount, including zero, when the gratuity is already in the total.

Why the IRS does not call it a tip

IRS Publication 531 gives the example almost word for word. A restaurant adds 18% for parties of six or more. The customer's bill for a party of eight includes that 18% on the tip line, and the total includes it. Because the customer did not have an unrestricted right to determine the amount, the 18% is a service charge. The employee does not put it in a tip diary. Service charges the employer pays out are wages, not tips.

Publication 531 lists four factors. A payment is a tip when it is made free from compulsion, the customer has the right to determine the amount, the payment is not negotiated or dictated by employer policy, and the customer generally has the right to determine who receives it. A sample calculation printed at the bottom of a check, with a blank line the guest can leave empty, is a tip. The IRS's second example in that publication is that case: suggested 15%, 18%, and 20% do not turn the payment into a service charge if the guest can enter any amount or nothing.

The third example is the one guests miss. Same mandatory 18%, plus an "additional tip" line where the guest writes 2%. Only that 2% is a tip. Cash you add on top is the tip. The 18% stays a service charge if the guest could not change it.

The IRS tip recordkeeping page says a mandatory large-party charge is not eligible for the qualified tips deduction, and that the employer may distribute service charges or keep them. Amounts it does pay out are wages on Form W-2, not tip-diary entries. A screen that forces a tip above zero is also listed there as a service charge. Fact Sheet #15 says the same thing for wage law: a compulsory charge is not a tip, it can count toward minimum wage and overtime if paid out, and it belongs in the regular rate used for overtime. Ask where the line goes before you add an extra. The dining room cannot see the payroll split.

When an extra amount makes sense

Add something when the service was better than a default 18% policy: a server who managed a complicated order, a long celebration, or a problem they fixed without being asked. A few dollars, or another 2% to 5% of the food, is enough to be legible. Another full 20% is a second tip, not a thank-you, unless you have done that math and you mean it.

Do not cross out a mandatory charge unless the restaurant says it is optional or it was applied in error. If the service was poor, ask a manager to adjust the charge they added.

Any extra you choose can stay on the pre-tax food total. The pre-tax guide shows why that gap grows on a large check.

Splitting it across the party

Once the gratuity is in the total, split the total, not the food alone. Everyone who ate owes a share of that charge. Treating the auto-gratuity as the cardholder's personal gift is how the person who volunteered a credit card subsidizes the table.

If you are itemizing because orders were far apart, apply the same 18% to each seat's food, or split the printed gratuity in proportion to each seat's food. Both methods land in the same place if the printed percentage matches the one you use. Shared bottles go on the seats that drank them. The split guide works through a four-person itemized example. Leftover cents should be rounded up per person so the cardholder is not short.

Say the policy when you sit down. A one-sentence warning prevents the "I only brought my share of the food" problem.

Worked example

Party of eight, $240 food, 18% included

Eighteen percent of $240 is $43.20. Food plus gratuity is $283.20, before tax. Eight people is $35.40 each, an even split with no leftover cent. If this 18% is already printed, do not add it again. Use the calculator at 18% only to see the charge, or set the tip to 0% and enter $283.20 if you are splitting a total that already includes it.

The double-tip version, for contrast: 20% of $240 added on top of the included $43.20 is another $48. Combined gratuity $91.20, which is 38% of the food. That is the tablet mistake. If you want a true extra 2% because the service was excellent, 2% of $240 is $4.80, about $0.60 per person. The custom-tip link is $240 split eight ways at 2%, and you would add that only on top of the printed 18%, not instead of a missing tip.
