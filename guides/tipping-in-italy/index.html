Tipping in Italy

By Matt Totty, TipSlice · Last reviewed October 19, 2026

A tip in Italy is not compulsory. Italia.it says there are no established rules, and that close to 10% of the bill is customary when you are satisfied. The Ministry of Education's study-in-Italy page says a tip is welcome but not an obligation. In Lazio, Regional Law 22/2019 forbids an added coperto charge.

Is a tip required in Italy?

No. Italia.it answers "Tipping in Italy: yes or no?" by saying tipping is not compulsory and there are no established rules, and that it is customary to leave an amount close to 10% of the bill when the customer is satisfied with the service. The education ministry's "Mance" section, written in Italian on that page, says tips are welcome but not an obligation. A tip is appreciation for good service, and unsatisfactory service is usually not rewarded. In a restaurant it goes directly to the waiter who served you, and it is not calculated as a preset percentage. In a bar, if you want to leave something, staff often put a small plate on the counter for coins.

What is the coperto?

The education ministry says most restaurants and pizzerias impose a fixed fee called coperto, and that people usually leave a few extra coins depending on the quality of the service, the type of restaurant, and the size of that coperto. The coperto is the fee. The coins are the tip. Neither that page nor Italia.it states a euro amount for the fee.

Lazio's rule is narrower, and it points the other way. Article 75, paragraph 4, of Regional Law 6 November 2019, n. 22 (Testo unico del commercio) says: "Nella somministrazione è vietato applicare costi aggiuntivi per il coperto." In food and drink service, additional charges for the coperto are forbidden. Rome is in Lazio, so that sentence is the statute for a Rome bill. It is a Lazio statute, so it is not the rule for Milan or Florence. The ministry's "most restaurants" sentence is the student page's national description. It is not a substitute for Article 75 on a check in Rome.

The same ban appears in the regional council's coordinated text of Law 22/2019, which is marked vigente. The regional PDF prints that section as Art. 75.

What does Lazio require on the price list?

Article 75 is a price-display rule, not a tip percent. Paragraph 1 says prices for food and drink service, including restaurants, must be posted on a table inside the premises. For restaurants, a table must also be readable from outside, excluding the wine list. Paragraph 2 says a fixed-price formula cannot add extra charges for service, and that the number and type of courses and drinks in that menu must be specified so the customer can see any extra cost. Paragraph 3 says that when service is at the table, the price list must be available before the customer orders, and it must indicate any service component so the price is clearly understandable.

Italia.it does not use the menu phrase "servizio incluso." The education ministry's tipping section does not use it either. Article 75 is the service rule quoted here: no extra service charge on a fixed-price menu, and any service component disclosed on the list before you order.

What does the bill have to show?

Italia.it says receipts and bills are obligatory. For any goods or services you buy, ask for the receipt or bill and keep it. All official commercial businesses must issue a payment slip. Displayed prices are the prices. Haggling is not the general custom, with an exception for fairs and markets. In early July and after the Christmas holidays, shops hold sales, and the dates vary by region. The currency is the euro. Coins run from 1 cent to €2. Notes run from €5 to €200. Cards listed on the page are Visa, MasterCard, American Express, Bancomat, Postamat, and PagoBancomat. In larger centres you can also pay by smartphone. Airports, large train stations, banks, and exchange agencies are where the page says you can convert currency.
