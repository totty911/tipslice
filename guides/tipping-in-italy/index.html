Tipping in Italy

By Matt Totty, TipSlice · Last reviewed October 19, 2026

A tip in Italy is not compulsory. Italia.it, the national tourism site, says there are no established rules, and that it is customary to leave an amount close to 10% of the bill when you are satisfied with the service. Italy's Ministry of Education, on its study-in-Italy page, says tips are appreciated but not an obligation, and that most restaurants and pizzerias charge a fixed fee called coperto. The two pages do not use the same number. Read both, then read the bill.

Is a tip required in Italy?

No. Italia.it answers "Tipping in Italy: yes or no?" by saying it is not compulsory. The education ministry's "Mance" section says a tip should be understood as appreciation for good service, and that unsatisfactory service is usually not rewarded. In a restaurant the tip goes directly to the waiter who served you, freely, and it is not calculated as a preset percentage. In a bar, if you want to leave something, staff often put a small plate on the counter for coins.

Travel Tipper marks Italy optional and starts Rome, Milan, and Florence at 0% because neither page makes the tip a charge. Type 10 when you are using the Italia.it "close to 10%" sentence. Leave zero when you are using the ministry's "not an obligation" sentence.

What is the coperto?

The education ministry says most restaurants and pizzerias impose a fixed fee called coperto, and that people usually leave a few extra coins depending on the quality of the service, the type of restaurant, and the size of that coperto. The coperto is the fee. The coins are the tip. Italia.it's tipping paragraph does not use the word coperto and does not give the fee a euro amount, so this page does not print one. Look for the line on the slip.

What does the bill have to show?

Italia.it says receipts and bills are obligatory. Businesses must issue a payment slip, and you should ask for it and keep it. Displayed prices are the prices. Haggling is not the general custom, with an exception for fairs and markets. Early July and after the Christmas holidays, shops hold sales, and the dates vary by region. The currency is the euro. Coins run from 1 cent to €2. Notes run from €5 to €200. Cards listed on the page include Visa, Mastercard, American Express, Bancomat, Postamat, and PagoBancomat. If a printed line is not the dishes and not a tip you chose, it is on that slip.

One country, three cities

Travel Tipper lists Rome, Milan, and Florence because they are major cities where a visitor is likely to have a bill, not because each has its own custom. Selecting any of them sets the currency to euros, sets the tip field to 0%, and shows the Italia.it note with the link. You can change the percent after that. A share link can lock the percent you chose. A dinner for two that you want left as the bill alone is Rome, €64, two people, 0%. The same bill with the customary satisfied-guest figure is Rome, €64, two people, 10%. Ten percent of €64 is €6.40. The total is €70.40, and each person’s share rounds up so the two shares cover the check.

If the euro total needs to be in dollars, the home-currency line uses the same mid-market table as the currency converter. That line names its source and time. It is not the amount your card will post. A terminal that offers to charge your home currency is using a different rate. Compare the two before you accept the terminal’s conversion.

Do not import a U.S. 20%

A sit-down tip in the United States is a different custom. The restaurant guide cites Emily Post’s 15% to 20% of the pre-tax bill for that setting. Italia.it does not cite that range, and it says there are no established rules. Using 20% because it is familiar is a choice you can still type. It is not what the Italian tourism page describes. Italy tipping FAQ

Is a tip required in Italy?

Italia.it says tipping in Italy is not compulsory and there are no established rules. It also says it is customary to leave an amount close to 10% of the bill when the customer is satisfied with the service.

Why does Travel Tipper start Rome at 0%?

Because the national tourism site says a tip is not compulsory. The note still tells you that close to 10% is customary when you are satisfied, and you can type that percent. Rome, Milan, and Florence share the same country note.

What is the coperto?

Italy's Ministry of Education says most restaurants and pizzerias charge a fixed fee called coperto. A tip is separate: appreciated, not an obligation, and not calculated as a preset percentage. Italia.it does not state a euro amount for the coperto.
