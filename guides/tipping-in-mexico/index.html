Tipping in Mexico

By Matt Totty, TipSlice · Last updated October 17, 2026

The source for this page is a government tourism FAQ, not a city blog. Mexico’s Ministry of Tourism, on the visitor FAQ at sectur.gob.mx, answers “Should I tip in Mexico?” this way: tipping is a common practice, and it is not mandatory. It is common to tip 10% to 15% in restaurants. Small tips are appreciated for services such as hotel housekeeping and tour guides. That answer is national. It does not give Mexico City one rate, Guadalajara another, and Cancún a third.

Common, and not mandatory

Those two words are why Travel Tipper marks Mexico as expected and still lets you change the number. “Common practice” is stronger than a country that says a tip is not expected. “Not mandatory” means the FAQ is not describing a charge the restaurant may demand. The range is 10% to 15% in restaurants. The calculator starts at 10%, the low end, so the field matches a figure the secretariat actually printed. If the service was the kind of meal for which you want the high end, type 15. The note on the result links the FAQ either way.

The same answer mentions small tips for hotel housekeeping and tour guides. It does not print a peso amount for those jobs. This guide will not invent one. A restaurant bill is the 10% to 15% sentence. A housekeeper or a guide is the “small tips are appreciated” sentence, and the amount is whatever you decide that phrase means on that day.

The FAQ also says the official currency is the Mexican peso, and that U.S. dollars are accepted in many tourist areas, with a recommendation to exchange currency for better rates. Paying a tip in a different currency from the bill is a practical mess this page does not need to solve. If the check is in pesos, the Travel Tipper line for Mexico City, Guadalajara, or Cancún is in pesos. The home-currency conversion, if you ask for one, uses the currency converter table and names that table. It is not your card’s posted charge.

A peso example

Take a 800 peso meal, two people, at the 10% starting point. The tip is 80 pesos. The total is 880 pesos, or 440 pesos each. At 15% the tip is 120 pesos and the total is 920 pesos, or 460 pesos each. Neither link adds a tax line, because this page does not have a sourced Mexican sales-tax table for those cities. If the bill adds a tax that is not already in the price, type it. The 10% link is Mexico City, 800 pesos, two people, 10%. The 15% link is Cancún, 800 pesos, two people, 15%. Cancún uses the same country note as Mexico City. The city only changes the label.

If a check already includes a gratuity, the FAQ quoted here does not describe that line. Do not add 10% on top of a charge you did not read. The service-charge checker is for separating a required line from an extra tip, using the IRS pages it cites. Those IRS pages are U.S. tax rules. They tell you how the United States treats a mandatory service charge. They are not a Mexican consumer statute.

What this page will not claim

It will not say that skipping a tip is rude in a particular neighborhood. The secretariat FAQ does not say that. It will not say that every beach club uses 15%. The FAQ says 10% to 15% is common in restaurants, and that the practice is not mandatory. A U.S. sit-down range of 15% to 20%, from the restaurant guide, is a different country’s custom. You can type 20 if you mean to. The Mexican source you are reading does not ask for it.

Tourism FAQs change. If the bill in your hand and this paragraph disagree, the bill is the amount you were charged. This is not legal advice for a worker in Mexico and not a promise about what a card will post.

Mexico tipping FAQ

How much do people tip in a Mexican restaurant?

Mexico's Ministry of Tourism says tipping is a common practice and is not mandatory. It is common to tip 10% to 15% in restaurants. Small tips are appreciated for hotel housekeeping and tour guides.

Why does the calculator start at 10% rather than 15%?

Ten percent is the low end of the 10% to 15% range on the tourism secretariat FAQ. It is a starting point, not a city rule. Mexico City, Guadalajara, and Cancún share it, and you can change the percent.
