How to split a bill fairly

By Matt Totty, TipSlice · Last updated October 17, 2026

An even split is fair when people ordered similar amounts. It is unfair when one person had steak and drinks and someone else had a salad, and it is how groups accidentally under-tip when everyone Venmos "their food" and forgets the tip. This page is the arithmetic of those choices: when to divide evenly, how to itemize, how tax and tip follow the food, and what to do with the leftover cents that appear whenever money is divided by three, six, or seven.

When an even split works

Use one number per person when orders are close, when you agreed to split before ordering, or when itemizing would cost more goodwill than dollars. A set-price menu is the clean case. If the table said "let's just split it" and then ordered freely, that agreement is what you are honoring.

Do the tip and the tax before you divide. Splitting the food four ways and then letting each person "throw in a few dollars" is how a 20% intention becomes 11% at the register. Pick the percentage first. The restaurant guide puts the U.S. sit-down range at 15% to 20% of the pre-tax bill, following the Emily Post Institute chart. Then divide food, tax, and tip together.

Here is an even split with clean inputs. The pre-tax bill is $124.50. The tip is 18%, which is $22.41. The total before tax is $146.91. Four people is $36.7275 each, which cannot be paid in cash. The next section on cents shows why TipSlice collects $36.73 from each person. The link at the bottom opens that exact split.

Itemizing without shorting the tip

Itemize when the spread is obvious: one person ordered the tasting menu, another ordered a salad and tap water, or a couple is on a budget and the rest of the table is not. Each person takes their own food and drinks, then adds the same tip percentage, then adds their share of tax.

Use the $124.50 table again, this time by seat:

The itemized shares add back to the same $146.91 as an even split of the whole check. What changed is who pays it. An even split would have asked the $23 salad to subsidize the $42 steak. If that subsidy was not agreed, itemize.

Tax follows the same proportions when you are tipping pre-tax. If sales tax on this check were $10.00, seat A would owe 42/124.50 of the tax, which is about $3.37, on top of $49.56. You can also divide tax evenly when the law of the table is "food is separate, tax and tip are shared." Say which rule you are using. The pre-tax versus post-tax guide is the longer version of the base. The short version: agree whether 18% applies to food only or to food plus tax, then apply that one rule to every seat.

Each seat can be run through the calculator on its own subtotal. Seat A is $42 at 18%. Seat D is $23 at 18%. Doing it that way keeps the percentage identical, which is what keeps the server's tip whole.

Shared plates and drinks

A shared appetizer or a bottle of wine is not one person's entree. Split those lines across the people who ate or drank them, then add each person's individual dishes. If all four people shared a $16 appetizer, add $4 to each seat before you take 18%. If only two people drank a $40 bottle, add $20 to each of those two seats. Leaving the bottle on the even-split pile after you itemized the food puts the cost back on the people who did not drink it.

Someone who did not drink should not be handed an even share of the wine unless the table agreed to one pool. Itemize instead.

Parents usually pay kids' meals. A dessert the table insisted on buying can be split. Say which rule you are using before the check arrives.

Leftover cents

Dollars divided by people rarely land on a cent. Three friends on a $50 bill with no tip owe $16.666... each. There is no $16.666 coin. Two honest fixes exist.

  • Round each share up to the next cent. Each person pays $16.67. Together that is $50.01, one cent over the bill. The extra cent can stay with the person who paid the card, or it can be a cent of extra tip. The table is never short at the register.
  • Give the remainder to one person. Two people pay $16.66 and one pays $16.68. The total is exact. Someone has to be the person with the extra pennies, and that discussion is often worth more than the pennies.

TipSlice uses the first method. In the calculator, each person's total is rounded up so the shares cover the check. On the $124.50 bill with an 18% tip and four people, $146.91 divided by 4 is $36.7275. Rounded up, each share is $36.73. Four times $36.73 is $146.92, which is one cent over $146.91. The results note on the bill splitter says so. Pay the rounded figure. Do not "correct" it back down in the chat or the cardholder eats the cent and sometimes more, once four people have each trimmed a little.

Peer-payment apps that only allow whole dollars create a larger version of the same problem. If the true share is $36.73, sending $37 is the round-up. Sending $36 leaves the cardholder short 73 cents per person. On a table of six that habit becomes several dollars, which is a quiet cut to the tip.

The person who pays the card

If one person puts the entire check on a card, everyone else owes food, tax, and tip. The tip is not a favor the cardholder throws in. Send the per-person figure that already includes it. The Emily Post range is a percentage of the meal, and it does not shrink because the reimbursement happened in an app an hour later.

Look at the receipt before you calculate. An automatic gratuity for a large party is already in the total. Set the tip field to zero, or add only an extra you actually want, then split that grand total. Adding 18% in the calculator on top of an 18% line already printed is a double tip. The large-party guide shows a party of eight where that mistake turns 18% into 38%.

Separate checks from the restaurant avoid this math. Ask at the start, not when the plates are cleared. Some kitchens will not split a check after the order is in, and some add a charge for separate checks. If they will, each person can tip on their own slip. If they will not, you are in the itemized or even-split path above.

Before anyone sends money

  1. Read the receipt for a gratuity that is already included.
  2. Choose even or itemized out loud, based on how far apart the orders were.
  3. Pick one tip percentage and one base, pre-tax or post-tax, for the whole table.
  4. Put shared dishes only on the seats that shared them.
  5. Round each reimbursement up to the next cent, or to the next dollar if the app forces it. Do not round down.
