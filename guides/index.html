Tipping guides

By Matt Totty, TipSlice · Last updated October 17, 2026

TipSlice is a set of calculators and these guides. The calculators do the cents. The guides answer the question the calculator cannot: which percentage, on which line of the receipt, for which kind of work. Each guide cites the page a fact came from, usually the Emily Post Institute, the IRS, the U.S. Department of Labor, or the Bureau of Labor Statistics, and ends with a link that opens a filled-in example. Matt Totty runs TipSlice. The guides are general information, not tax or legal advice.

Three questions before you pick a percent

Most tipping arguments are one of these, and they have different answers.

  1. What kind of service was it? A sit-down meal in the United States is a 15% to 20% decision on the pre-tax bill. A coffee counter or a simple takeout order is often optional. A haircut is 15% to 20% of the service, not of the bottle of product. Delivery needs a percentage and a dollar floor, because 15% of a small order can be less than the trip cost. The service table is the short version of that split.
  2. Is money already on the bill? An 18% automatic gratuity for a party of eight is usually mandatory, and the IRS treats it as a service charge rather than a tip you still owe. A delivery fee or a service fee on an app is not the driver's tip. Read the label before you add a second amount. The large-party guide and the delivery guide go line by line.
  3. What number are you multiplying? Etiquette uses the pre-tax food and drink total. Card screens often use the after-tax total. On an $80 meal at New York City's 8.875% combined rate, those two 20% tips differ by $1.42. The pre-tax guide shows both calculator links.

If you are splitting, add a fourth question: even, or by seat? Even is fair when orders match. Itemizing is fair when they do not. Either way, divide food, tax, and tip together, and round each person's share up so the person who paid the card is not short. That arithmetic, including a $124.50 check that leaves a leftover cent, is the splitting guide.

How the examples open

Share links use a few query keys. b is the bill, p is people, t is the tip percent, and x is the sales-tax percent. When x is present, tax is on. o=1 tips on the after-tax total, and it only applies with x. A sit-down example for two people on $86.40 at 20% is https://tipslice.com/?b=86.40&p=2&t=20. The same keys work on the bill splitter. Nothing in the link is uploaded. The math runs in the browser, which the privacy policy explains.

Preset buttons on the tip calculator are 10%, 15%, 18%, 20%, and 25%. Any other t value opens the custom field. The percentage table is the right tool when you want every common rate on one bill before you choose. The sales tax calculator adds a rate or backs tax out of a total. Use it when the receipt folded the subtotal away and you still want a pre-tax tip. A bill that is not in your home currency goes to the currency converter, which pairs with the travel guide.

What these pages will not do

They will not invent a single percentage for every city. House policies, state tipped wages, and sales-tax rates differ. The Department of Labor's tipped-wage table, cited in the restaurant and tip-pool guides, is the place to see that a $2.13 federal cash wage is not the wage in California or Washington. They will not tell a server that a particular tip-out percentage is "standard." Federal law does not set one. The tip-pool guide labels its dollar example as an illustration for that reason.

They also will not treat a travel chart as a law. The travel guide quotes national tourism offices and government pages, then tells you to read the check for a service charge that overrides the chart. France's consumer authority says a tip cannot be demanded and that displayed prices include service. Japan's tourism organization says tipping is not customary and can cause discomfort. Those are different instructions from a U.S. dinner, and the guide keeps them different.

If a figure here disagrees with your receipt, the receipt wins. If a wage answer matters to a real paycheck, use the linked Department of Labor or IRS page, or your state labor agency. A path through the set

If you are at a restaurant tonight, read restaurants, then open the calculator. If the party is big enough that the menu mentioned a gratuity, read large parties first so you do not add 18% twice. If you are sending money to friends afterward, use the split guide. If you work the shift rather than pay it, start with tip-out and tip pooling. Travel planning starts with the country comparison, not with the U.S. 20% button.
